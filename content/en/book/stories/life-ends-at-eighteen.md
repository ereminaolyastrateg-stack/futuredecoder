I wish I had kept a diary.

Although, honestly, what would I have written at fourteen?

“Cleaned the classroom for money again today because my classmates are too squeamish to touch a mop. The bucket is heavy, the water is grey, and the boys pretend I don’t exist. Mood: fine.”

I was an ordinary outsider in an ordinary class. The daughter of an alcoholic and a kindergarten teacher. Cheap tights that bunched around my knees. A little overweight. A little feisty. Almost no interest from the boys in my class.

All in all, the standard package.

Nothing special.

School came easily to me. I loved mathematics almost physically — that feeling when a problem first stands in front of you like a wall, and you circle it, get angry, chew on your pen, and then suddenly something clicks and the wall turns out to be a door. I especially liked Skanavi’s problem book: its problems were not the usual school exercises, not “plug the numbers into the formula,” but real problems. The kind that made you think.

But I never crammed. I passed an exam, exhaled, and forgot everything.

Why remember it once the exam was over?

Except for English.

I hated English.

I don’t remember when it lost me — or when I lost it. But by ninth grade, it was clear: I was hopelessly behind. I did not understand the tenses. I could not make out the words. I could not learn them. I hated the teacher, I hated the textbook, and I hated those cassette tapes with the upbeat voices of people who somehow knew how to speak a language created specifically to humiliate me.

My logic was ironclad.

In the early 2000s, you couldn’t get in anywhere without English.
No university meant no life.
No life meant the end.

I hammered in the final nail myself in tenth grade, when tutors came to our school to prepare students for the Higher School of Economics.

They were from another world. Confident, fast, carrying folders, handouts, and a normal Moscow future in their hands. They talked about entrance exams as if they were simply a route: turn here, study this, pay here, get admitted there.

I had no money for tutors.

So HSE was out of reach.

So everything was out of reach.

Good luck, Olya.

I bought a university guide. A thick book with thin grey pages, where an entire life had been sorted into departments, admission scores, and entrance exams. In the evenings, I leafed through it and put check marks next to everything that seemed interesting: economics, mathematics, something about management, something international.

And almost everywhere, I ran into the same word.

English.

It was not a subject. It was a barrier gate.

I cried over the guide, closed it, and put it on a shelf. A very grown-up decision: if a door is closed, pretend you never wanted to go through it.

It is worth pausing here.

While I was writing off my entire life, my chemistry teacher, Larisa Nikolaevna, would periodically tell me that I was exceptionally capable. Her husband, a physicist, said much the same: you have a good head on your shoulders; go somewhere you can study physics.

I did not hear them.

It was as if they were speaking the very English I hated.

Everyone in my class went to university. Those who had money for tutors. Those who did not. Those who knew exactly where they were going. And those who seemed to go along simply because everyone else was going.

Everyone.

Except me.

I went to a tax college in the neighbouring district.

I graduated from the mathematics track of a strong academic secondary school with good teachers — and went to a tax college because it did not require English.

There was something almost perfectly absurd about it. A person who loved difficult problems chose not a problem to solve, but a place where no one would ask her about irregular verbs.

My chemistry teacher urged me to try Moscow State University.

I did not try.

My two years at college passed like a life I had supposedly chosen myself but kept watching from the outside. Tests, lecture notes, tidy notebooks, conversations about a future job in the tax service. Then came a single day of practical training at a tax office: corridors, folders, people’s faces in a queue, women behind desks who already knew something about life that I did not want to know.

And the unmistakable realization: this was not for me.

Next came the newspaper Iz Ruk v Ruki — From Hand to Hand. It was thick, with tiny print that made my eyes ache. I circled job listings with a pen, called the numbers, and tried to sound like an adult. Back then, I still thought of work as a door you had to knock on in just the right way: if someone opened it, everything after that would somehow sort itself out.

The door was opened not by the newspaper but by a neighbour from our dacha, a lucky connection. She found me a job at Vnesheconombank.

Eight thousand roubles. A big bank. Accounting.

There was a desk, a computer, piles of documents, and grown women who knew how to live among check marks, payment orders, journal entries, and endless bits of paper as if this were a perfectly normal habitat. They knew where to put a signature, where to make a copy, who should receive a folder, and why you could not simply pick up this number and write it somewhere else.

On my first day, I understood: I am not an accountant.

But the first realization rarely releases a person from a job.

The check marks and payment orders stretched into two years.

Then came NOMOS-Bank. Reporting. That was more interesting. A system began to emerge behind the numbers: ratios, constraints, the logic of a bank, its internal skeleton. Instead of merely moving data from one place to another, I could understand why it had to be moved at all.

For the first time, I felt that work could be not only an exercise in patience but also a way of deciphering how the world works.

Within a year, I had learned everything there was to learn.

And once again, the question appeared: where next?

Next came VTB. The strategy department.

They hired me after a strange interview in which they asked how many petrol stations there were in Moscow and how many ping-pong balls could fit inside an aeroplane. Of course, I did not know the exact answers. No one did. That was the point.

I was not supposed to recall the correct answer.

I was supposed to think out loud.

And this was an entirely different world.

For a long time, I could not understand how it had happened.

A strong academic secondary school. The mathematics track. A perfectly good brain. Teachers who saw more in me than I saw in myself. Larisa Nikolaevna, who told me, “Try Moscow State University.” Her physicist husband, who also thought I should go somewhere with physics, with problems, somewhere I could use my head.

And yet — a tax college in the neighbouring district.

Not because I dreamt of the tax system.
Not because I had always wanted to sit at a desk with folders.
Not because I had discovered a passionate interest in VAT.

But because it did not require English.

This is what choosing your destiny sometimes looks like. Not a grand dramatic gesture, not an “I have decided to dedicate my life to…” but a small, almost technical constraint: English required here, not required there. So I go where it is not required.

Years later, I learned that my husband had enrolled at Bauman Moscow State Technical University without English. He simply applied — and got in. He took the exams he needed to take. He followed his own route.

I remember the strange feeling: as if someone had opened a door into a room that had been next to me all along but did not exist on my map.

It turned out I could have done it.

I simply did not know I could.

And the most painful part is that it was not the only moment like that.

In ninth grade, I had thought about medicine too. At one point, I very much wanted to become a dentist. There was something clear about it: a specific profession, working with your hands, precision; someone comes to you in pain, and you help. Not the abstract idea of “getting in somewhere,” but real work.

But I quickly stopped myself.

I thought the entrance exams included either physics or chemistry. I no longer remember exactly what confused me, but I remember the conclusion perfectly: I would never pass chemistry.

And that was that.

Not “I need to find out.”
Not “I need to ask someone.”
Not “Which universities are there, which exams do they require, and what are my options?”

No.

I would never pass chemistry — so I would not become a dentist.

The investigation into my future was complete.

Now it seems almost funny. A grown woman looks back and sees a girl standing before a vast system of education, professions, cities, universities, exams, and possible paths — making a decision on the basis of a vague rumour in her own head.

But it was not funny then.

Then, it looked like reality.

I heard the story of how “it could have been different” many years later — from my dentist. We started talking while she was drilling my tooth, as so often happens with compulsory conversations: you are lying there with your mouth open, a drill buzzing inside it, and someone suddenly starts telling you something important about life.

She had wanted to be a dentist too.

And for her, as for me, Moscow had seemed almost impossible: expensive, fiercely competitive, dependent on connections and preparation, full of people from families where everyone already knew which path to take and how to get in. All the things that usually lit up a sign in my head: “Not for you.”

But she did not close the guide.

She did not decide that if Moscow was difficult, the dream was cancelled.

She simply asked a different question.

Not: “Why won’t it work for me?”
But: “Where could it still be possible?”

She found a medical university in another city. She got in. She completed her degree. She came back. She opened her own practice.

And there she was, standing over me in a white coat, holding an instrument and treating my tooth — while I lay there thinking, wait. You mean this was possible too?

The same problem. Two different questions.

I asked:
“Why won’t it work for me?”

She asked:
“How can I still get what I want?”

And that, it seems, was one of the most important differences between our paths.

Not talent.
Not our starting conditions.
Not even money.

The difference was the question that began the search.

Back then, I did not even know that the second question existed.
