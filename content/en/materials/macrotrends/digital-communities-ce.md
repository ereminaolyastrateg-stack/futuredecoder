*[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities|Digital Communities]] is a macrotrend about how the network turned from a way of talking into a form of power. This is its version in the **[[en/four-worlds/centralized-earth|Centralized — Earth]]** world, where communities live in a shared frame held by the state and institutions.*

## 2035. The neighbourhood chat

Natalia moderates her neighbourhood chat in the national messenger. Through the chat residents choose where to put a new sports ground, sign children up for clubs and learn about water cuts. Once a year the city collects proposals for its development programme there: last time twelve thousand came in, and three made it into the plan. Natalia likes that everything is in one place and that the chat has official status. Her son Artyom plays for the school team, and the team's supporters are a community of their own, with a kit, an anthem and trips to matches. Sometimes Artyom complains that his favourite game server "doesn't work" and everyone has moved to the permitted app. It's calmer there, says Natalia. It's more boring there, says Artyom.

## Where we come from

Communities always lived next to institutions: the parish, the school, the factory, the club, the city. Digital networks briefly took them outside the frame of states. Now states are bringing them back into their frame — through their own infrastructure, participation in shared plans and values. [[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities#where-we-are-now|More — history and figures]].

## What the world becomes

In CE a digital community is part of public infrastructure. It is tied to institutions — school, sport, the city, a brand — and sustains long-term ties around shared values and belonging. The state builds its own communication infrastructure, collects citizens' ideas within a frame it sets itself, and thinks about technological sovereignty. The community gains protection and recognition, but acts within the rules.

## What it gives and what it costs

**What it gives.** Security and a shared frame: it is clear who is responsible, where to turn, what is allowed. The community is recognised and can influence decisions.

**What it costs.** The frame is set in advance. You don't choose the platforms and the rules, and stepping outside the frame is costly.

## What was said at the lecture

### The state doesn't see micro-communities

The main problem of big systems: they can't see what happens in small groups.

> The state doesn't see what is happening in micro- and nano-subcultural groups.

## What you can do now

1. Find out what official channels of participation your city has: surveys, participatory budgets, public councils.
2. If you run a community, understand what rules it lives under and what happens if the platform closes.
3. Link your community to a local institution — a school, a library, a sports club — so it has support outside the network.
4. Put ideas where they are collected, and check what became of them.

## Digital Communities in other worlds

The worlds differ in who holds the rules of a community:

- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-cs|CS]] — the platform.** The algorithm and the owner decide whom you see.
- **CE — the state.** Communities live in a shared frame and national infrastructure.
- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-ds|DS]] — the creator.** Your own server, your own subscription, your own rules.
- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-de|DE]] — the members.** A community without a sponsor, where everyone is the sponsor.
