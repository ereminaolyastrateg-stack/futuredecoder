*[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities|Digital Communities]] is a macrotrend about how the network turned from a way of talking into a form of power. This is its version in the **[[en/four-worlds/decentralized-synthetic|Decentralized — Synthetic]]** world, where the creator holds the rules: author, host, server owner.*

## 2035. Your own server

Timofey runs a community of board-game lovers: two thousand people on his own server, which sits in his flat, and a subscription for a token fee. Moderation is helped by an open model he fine-tuned on the club's rules himself. Timofey decides whom to let in, which topics are closed and where the money goes. Members value that there are no ads and no feed deciding for them. On Friday he comes down with a fever, the server is down for two days, and half the chat panics: nobody but Timofey knows how it all works. On Monday he writes a post: "I need a second admin." Three people reply, and for the first time Timofey thinks his community is not only his.

## Where we come from

When the feeds of big platforms started to steer attention, authors and creators began moving to direct channels: newsletters, subscriptions, donations, their own servers. Open AI models made it possible to run your own tools without someone else's cloud. [[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities#where-we-are-now|More — history and figures]].

## What the world becomes

In DS a community gathers around a creator and their rules. Direct channels, subscriptions and donations return control over attention, monetisation and rules. Technology — your own server, your own model, your own agent — lets you do without big platforms. But the community rests on one person: their energy, honesty and health.

## What it gives and what it costs

**What it gives.** Control over attention and income. No one else's algorithm; a direct link to people who chose to come.

**What it costs.** Everything rests on one person. The creator gets tired, ill or makes mistakes — and the community risks falling apart. It is hard for one person to carry both the infrastructure and the people.

## What was said at the lecture

### You can only understand a community from inside

Matt Klein's method: trends are born in small subcultures, and looking at them from outside is useless.

> You can't understand a community without being a member of it.

### "I'm right, and you do as you like"

Many small communities hold together on the creator's certainty of being right rather than on agreement.

### One person can't carry it

The DS world is deflating: one person can't carry physical resources alone, and people drift into scenarios with other people.

> physical resources can't be carried alone, so this scenario is shifting into other scenarios

<!-- Same source, section on the DS world. -->

## What you can do now

1. If you run a community, appoint a second admin and write down how everything works.
2. Keep a copy of the community's data yourself, not only on the platform.
3. Try an open model for one community task: moderation, search, answers to common questions.
4. Once a year ask members which rules they are ready to hold together with you.

## Digital Communities in other worlds

The worlds differ in who holds the rules of a community:

- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-cs|CS]] — the platform.** The algorithm and the owner decide whom you see.
- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-ce|CE]] — the state.** Communities live in a shared frame and national infrastructure.
- **DS — the creator.** Your own server, your own subscription, your own rules.
- **[[en/materials/macrotrends/digital-communities-de|DE]] — the members.** A community without a sponsor, where everyone is the sponsor.
