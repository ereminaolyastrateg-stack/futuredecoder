*As machines can do more and more, people set out to prove that their own skill is worth something: not the amount of work done, but its quality, meaning and human contribution. This macrotrend is about what it means to be a master next to AI.*

## Four worlds

What sets a master apart in each world is who they listen to:

| World | Who the master listens to | What mastery is | The price |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[en/materials/macrotrends/human-mastery-cs\|CS]] | The instruction | Executing what exists, precisely | Boredom and signing without reading |
| [[en/materials/macrotrends/human-mastery-ce\|CE]] | The shared goal | Finding your place in a big common cause | You don't choose the goal or the path |
| [[en/materials/macrotrends/human-mastery-ds\|DS]] | Yourself | Creating what doesn't exist yet | No guarantee anyone needs it |
| [[en/materials/macrotrends/human-mastery-de\|DE]] | The environment | Constantly changing what exists, together with people | An endless process, and fatigue |

A second axis: CS and DE work with what already exists (one executes it, the other reworks it), while DS and CE create something new (one for themselves, the other for a shared goal). The worlds are connected: CS needs people with a DE way of seeing at its edges, where automation stops working.

## Where we are now

### The word "master"

The word comes from the Latin *magister*, "the one who is greater, the teacher". In medieval guilds the path was strict. An apprentice served a master for years without pay, then became a journeyman paid by the day (from the French *journée*) and moved from workshop to workshop. He became a master only when the guild accepted his test piece, the *masterpiece* (German *Meisterstück*, French *chef-d'œuvre*). Many stayed journeymen for life. A master was not simply a skilled person, but someone recognised by other masters and granted the right to teach.

Two things matter in this history. Mastery was always a **path**: from simple tasks to complex ones, next to someone who knows more. And it was always **recognition**: someone decided that you were a master now.

### Four waves of automation

This is not the first round. Each wave took one layer of mastery away from people and forced a new answer to the question of what mastery is now.

1. **Hands (19th century).** Machines took over craft: looms replaced weavers and spinners, and skilled masters became machine operators. The Luddites who smashed the machines remain the symbol of that break.
2. **Operations (early 20th century).** The assembly line and Taylor's "scientific management" cut craft into simple operations. Mastery moved from the workshop floor to the office that designs the work.
3. **Calculation (second half of the 20th century).** Computers took routine mental work: arithmetic, bookkeeping, typing, drafting. Those who set the task and interpret the result became more valuable.
4. **The first draft (since 2022).** Generative AI produces first versions of texts, code, analysis and images — exactly the work newcomers used to learn on.

Each time, automation broke not so much mastery itself as the **ladder to it**. Today this is especially visible: it is not experienced specialists who are hit, but the first rung.

### The numbers

- **The entry to professions is narrowing.** According to the Stanford Digital Economy Lab (August 2026, ADP payroll data), since late 2022 employment of 22–25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI has fallen by about 11%, while in the least exposed ones it grew by 10%. The gap is about 19%. People are not being laid off; fewer are being hired. Experienced workers in the same occupations, especially those relying on practical, hands-on knowledge, are doing better. The authors stress that this is a correlation, not a proven cause. [Source](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- **Skills go out of date fast.** Employers surveyed by the World Economic Forum expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030. Over the same period, 170 million jobs are expected to appear and 92 million to disappear. [Source](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/)

### The question the scenarios grow from

If the machine makes the first draft and the ladder to mastery is broken, what is human mastery now, how do you reach it, and who recognises it? The key tensions up to 2035:

- living mastery, or its replacement by an algorithm;
- real learning, or a "box-ticking simulator";
- responsibility for quality, or "generated it and handed it in".

The four worlds answer this question differently — that is the [table above](#four-worlds).
