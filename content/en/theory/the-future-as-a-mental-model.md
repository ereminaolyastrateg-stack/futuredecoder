*In short: our decisions are steered not only by facts but by an image of the future, an inner map of what is possible "for people like me". That map is assembled early, out of fears and borrowed templates, and it keeps working long after it has stopped matching reality. It can be updated, and it starts with changing the question we ask.*

## What was really going on

Here is the good news: I was not a stupid girl. I was a very predictable one.

When I look back now at the story of English, the university guide, the tax college and the dentistry career that never happened, I no longer want to explain it only by poverty, a bad start or the lack of tutors. All of that mattered, of course. Money, family, neighbourhood and access to information are not decorative circumstances but real forces that narrow a path. But my story had another layer, less visible and perhaps more important: I was not testing reality. I was testing my fear.

Daniel Kahneman described two systems of thinking: fast and slow. The fast system reacts almost automatically. It recognises the familiar, fills in the picture from the first clues, saves effort, dislikes uncertainty and very quickly says: "That's dangerous", "That's not for people like us", "Better stay away". The slow system can doubt, check, calculate and look for another option, but it takes effort. It needs someone inside to hit the brakes and say: wait, do we actually know this?

In tenth grade I lived on the fast system. It saw English and instantly pulled out of memory everything connected with it: shame at the blackboard, a teacher I hated, the feeling of falling behind, cheerful foreign voices on cassettes, a textbook I opened already surrendering. English was not a subject for me; it was the smell of defeat. And as soon as it appeared next to any future, that future was automatically closed.

The first bias here is the availability heuristic. The brain often judges not the real probability of an event but how easily it can recall a similar example. If the example is vivid, painful and emotionally charged, it starts to feel like proof. I did not know that you could not get into a good university without English. I did not check all the options. I simply remembered my humiliation with English very easily, and that was enough to draw a conclusion about the whole education system.

In business this happens all the time. A company once had a bad experience with a new sales channel, say a marketplace: it lost money and got chaos with logistics. A year later someone says in a meeting: "Marketplaces don't work for us." Formally it sounds like experience. In fact it is often one painful episode that has become the map of the whole territory. Nobody goes back to work out what actually failed: the range, the price, the product pages, the team, the warehouse, the contract, the timing. What remains in memory is simple: it hurt, so we don't go there.

It was the same with me. English was my failed marketplace.

The second bias is confirmation bias. Once the brain has formed the conclusion "I won't get in", it starts collecting evidence for exactly that conclusion. Not because the person deliberately lies to themselves, but because attention is selective. I opened the university guide and did not see a multitude of routes, faculties, cities and exam options. I saw one recurring word: English. Everything that confirmed my version lit up. Everything that might have disproved it simply never entered my field of vision.

I could have asked my teachers. I could have gone to admissions offices. I could have looked at universities in other cities. I could have found out where English was not required, where another subject could be taken instead, where there were preparatory courses, where the competition was lower, where the route was harder but still possible. But to do that I would first have had to doubt my own verdict. And I did not doubt it. I was looking not for a way out but for proof that there was none.

In business it looks like this: an owner is sure that "customers aren't buying anything right now" and from then on notices only the facts that prove falling demand. One client turned us down: see, everything is bad. A competitor is dumping prices: see, the market is dead. A manager says the leads are cold: see, people won't pay. Meanwhile the same customers may be buying from another brand, in another format, with a differently packaged value, but that no longer makes it into the picture. When a person is sure there is no demand, they stop exploring where it is.

The third bias is hasty generalisation. In ninth grade I thought about dentistry, but somewhere inside a vague thought appeared that you had to take either chemistry or physics. I did not look into it. I did not go and find out. I did not ask people who had actually applied. I did not check different universities. I simply took a badly formed feeling and turned it into a law: I won't pass chemistry, so I won't be a dentist.

Now it seems almost funny. A girl stands in front of a huge system of professions, cities, exams, preparatory courses, medical schools and possible detours, and makes a decision based on fog in her head. But that is exactly how important decisions are often made. Not on the basis of data, but on the basis of a fragment that was never checked in time.

Companies do the same. "We don't need TikTok, it's all kids there." "Our customers don't buy online." "You can't offer instalments in the premium segment." "People here don't pay for subscriptions." "Women over forty aren't interested in technology." "Spaniards will never go to a banya." Each of these phrases can sound like experience, but inside it there is often not research but a hasty generalisation. One example, one market, one acquaintance, one failure, one old picture, and on that basis a whole direction is closed.

The fourth bias is the anchoring effect. English became my anchor. I attached myself to it as the main condition of the future and then judged every decision through it. Not "what do I want?", not "what are my strengths?", not "what routes are there?", but "where won't they ask me about English?". So I chose not a direction but a place where I could avoid running into my fear.

In business the anchor can be an old margin, an old product, an old client, an old sales model. A company once made good money on one format and then spends years trying to bring exactly that back, even though the market has moved on. Not because it is objectively the best path, but because an internal logic has been built around the old success: this is the real thing, and everything else is temporary, frivolous, not ours. An anchor is convenient because it creates a sense of certainty. But it also keeps you from seeing that the boat has long been drifting in another direction.

The fifth bias is learned helplessness. This is no longer just an error of thinking but a state in which a person stops checking whether action is possible, because they have run into the feeling "I can't" too many times. If there are few adults around who can show routes, if the family has no experience of applying to university, if money is always the limit, if school does not explain the system but only sorts children by grades and means, then at some point a person starts saving energy on attempts. They do not fight the closed door. They choose in advance the corridor with fewer doors.

And here it matters: cognitive biases do not mean "you're stupid". On the contrary, they often show that the brain is working normally. It saves energy, protects from pain, looks for the familiar, does not want to end up again where it was ashamed. The problem begins where a protective mechanism becomes a navigation system. What was meant to protect me from an unpleasant experience ended up narrowing my life.

This is how an image of the future is formed.

We often think an image of the future is something beautiful and deliberate: a dream, a strategy, a plan, a vision board, a list of goals for the year. But more often it is built much deeper and more simply. An image of the future is our inner idea of what is possible at all. Not in the world in general, but for us specifically. For people like us, from a family like ours, with our money, our body, our English, from our neighbourhood, with our biography.

It answers questions we do not always hear: where I am allowed to go and where I am not; what is realistic for me and what is laughable; what I have the right to aim for and where I had better not embarrass myself; whom I can believe and who is not talking about my life; which doors are really closed and which I simply don't know how to open.

And the most unpleasant part: a large part of this map is assembled quite early. The psychologist Jeffrey Arnett described the period of emerging adulthood, roughly from the late teens to the mid-twenties. It is a time when a person actively explores work, relationships and worldview, tries on different versions of themselves and collects the first serious answers to the question "what life is possible for me". But these answers often appear before we have a mature ability to test them. We are already drawing conclusions about the world, but we are not yet very good at separating fact from family belief, someone else's fear from a real limit, local experience from a law of life.

Then a person grows up. They have more information, more experience, more opportunities. But the old map does not disappear automatically. It keeps working in the background. Someone can be a forty-year-old manager, an experienced entrepreneur, a well-educated professional, and still, in some places, act according to a map assembled at seventeen.

You have surely seen people like this. Smart, strong, with resources, but inside them an old formula still lives: "only a corporation is safe", "business is for people with connections", "big money is never made honestly", "it's too late to start after forty", "if it didn't work once, it's not for me". They may read new research, look at new markets, agree that the world has changed, and still act as if they were in the old reality.

Toffler called this future shock: the world changes faster than the human psyche can adapt to it. But in personal life and in business I think it often looks subtler. A person is not necessarily afraid of the future as a science-fiction catastrophe. They simply keep using an old instruction manual in a new environment. As if they had moved to another city but were trying to get around with a map of their old neighbourhood.

My map was Vykhino, a district on the outskirts of Moscow, in the early 2000s. Family experience, a lack of money, the school hierarchy, the absence of adult guides, the university guide, the fear of English, the feeling that a normal path was for people with a different start. This map was not the whole truth about the world. It was the local truth of my environment. For survival it may even have helped: don't go where you'll be humiliated; choose what is available; don't waste energy on the impossible. But as a map of the future it was far too narrow.

Here Goldratt and his Theory of Constraints help me a lot. He wrote about production systems, but the principle transfers surprisingly well to thinking: the whole system moves at the speed of its narrowest link. You can improve individual parts, strengthen the team, hire people, invest in advertising, study, work harder, but if the main constraint has not been found, the whole system will keep stalling in the same place.

In business this is easy to see. A company can endlessly improve its website, hire a social media specialist, change the packaging, launch new promotions, but if its bottleneck is not traffic but customer distrust, growth will not happen. You can increase production, but if the bottleneck is in sales, the warehouse will just fill up. You can train salespeople in scripts, but if the owner is not ready to reconsider the product itself, scripts will not help. A system does not speed up where it is convenient for us to improve it. It speeds up where the real constraint is.

The same goes for a person. The narrowest link may not be ability, discipline or even money. The narrowest link may be the image of the future.

If I don't see an option, I can't choose it. If I don't know you can get into university without English, that possibility does not exist for me. If I don't know you can become a dentist by studying in another city, the other city never appears on the map. If I am sure that "people like me" don't get into strong universities, good companies and interesting professions, I will look not for the best route but for the safest way to avoid defeat.

And this is not only my story. An entrepreneur may not launch a new product not because the market is definitely not ready, but because their inner map says: "We're small, that's not for us." A team may not go international not because there is no chance, but because the founder still has the old English barrier inside. A woman may not aim for a partnership, investment or a big role not because she couldn't handle it, but because her image of the future does not contain that version of herself. A country may reproduce the same institutions for decades not because there are no alternatives, but because the collective imagination does not allow for a different arrangement.

An image of the future rarely lives in the head as a rigorous theory. More often it lives as a metaphor. "English is a barrier." "Moscow is not for us." "You can't get anywhere without connections." "Stability means only the state or a big corporation." "A bird in the hand." "If we never lived well, there's no point starting now." "The main thing is not to stick your neck out." Such phrases do not look like decisions, but they are exactly what often drives decisions.

A metaphor is dangerous because it does not argue. It simply sets the frame. If the future is a forest, you must enter it with caution. If the future is a market, you must bargain. If the future is an exam, you must guess the right answer. If the future is a war, you must defend yourself. If the future is a closed club, you must find a pass or resign yourself. If the future is a space of possibilities, you must explore. The same reality under different metaphors produces different actions.

Business lives by metaphors too. Some companies say "we are fighting for the market", and then they have enemies, fronts, territory to capture, mobilisation and a rigid hierarchy. Others say "we are building an ecosystem", and they start thinking about connections, partners, retention, environments and long relationships. Others still say "we are a content factory", and gradually start measuring everything by volume of output, even if the audience has long needed not a factory but trust. A metaphor does not just decorate speech. It quietly designs management decisions.

Fred Polak wrote that societies live not only by facts but by images of the future. If the future seems open, people act one way. If it seems closed, they act differently. This applies to states, companies, families and one teenager with a university guide on her lap. An image of the future can be stronger than statistics, because statistics have to be understood, while an image is already at work.

Jens Beckert wrote about fictional expectations in the economy: people invest money, build companies, buy assets and enter markets not because the future is already known, but because they believe in a particular picture of it. An investor buys not only current profit but a story of growth. A founder hires a team not for today's demand but for an expected market. A buyer takes on a flat not only as square metres but as faith in a neighbourhood, a country, safety, growth and their own future way of life.

And here you can clearly see that an image of the future is not a daydream on the sofa. It becomes an economic force. If many people believe property always goes up, they buy property, and for a while that demand really does sustain the growth. If many believe artificial intelligence will replace whole professions, companies start restructuring budgets, people go to learn new tools, investors pour money into AI startups and universities change their programmes. Even if the specific forecasts later turn out to be exaggerated, the actions have already been taken. A future people believed in starts to change the present.

The same works in the negative direction. If entrepreneurs believe that "you can't plan anything in this country", they don't build long projects. If employees believe that "any initiative will be punished", they don't propose ideas. If young people believe that "a good life won't happen anyway", they choose short pleasures over long investments in themselves. If parents believe that "the world is only getting more dangerous", they teach their children not to explore but to hide. And then we wonder why the system does not develop, even though formally the resources are there.

This is the self-fulfilling prophecy. Not in the mystical sense that a thought attracted an event, but in a very practical one: people start behaving as if a certain future were already inevitable, and through their actions bring exactly that future closer. I decided there was no decent future without English and chose a path that confirmed it. A company decides its customers are not ready to pay more and never tests the product value they might pay for. A society decides people are incapable of self-organisation and builds institutions in which people really do forget how to organise themselves.

Sohail Inayatullah calls one such mechanism the "used future": when we live not by our own image but by someone else's template, inherited from family, school, culture, profession, media or past experience. This future was invented by someone before us. It can look like tradition, common sense, adult wisdom or realism, but its main feature is that we have never checked whether it fits us.

My used future sounded roughly like this: people without money have few options; if a strong university requires English, a strong university is not for me; if a subject doesn't come easily, avoid everything where it appears; if a door looks closed, better not knock. It was not my conscious choice. It was a set of other people's fears and my own, speaking in my voice.

That is why the story of the dentist struck me so much. She had similar constraints: Moscow, competition, money, a hard admission process, a profession you don't just walk into from the street. But her inner map contained a different question. Not "why won't it work for me?" but "where is it possible after all?". That question did not remove the difficulties. It did not make the world kind, easy and fair. It simply did not let the first closed door become the whole of reality.

This, I think, is the main point of working with an image of the future. Not to draw yourself a pretty picture and believe that everything will definitely work out. That is too naive. And not to deny constraints. Constraints are real. But between a constraint and a verdict there is a space for checking. In that space a person can see an alternative route, another market, another role, another city, another model, another ally, another way of acting.

An image of the future is not something you simply have. It has to be updated regularly.

The brain will build the future out of past experience anyway. It cannot do otherwise. If we don't do it consciously, it takes the most available material: family fears, traumatic episodes, old social norms, other people's expectations, the news, conversations around us, the failures we remember most vividly. Out of this it assembles a map by which we then call some decisions reasonable and others impossible.

But the map can be revised. You can ask: where did this image come from? Who gave it to me? What time was it made for? What facts confirm it? What facts contradict it? Which options am I not seeing because they don't fit my metaphor? Where am I calling reality what is really an old defensive reaction?

That is why the second question matters so much.

Not "why won't it work for me?" but "how might it be possible after all?"

Not "why has the market died?" but "where has demand moved?"

Not "why aren't people buying?" but "what are they already paying for, just not to us?"

Not "why isn't the team taking initiative?" but "what system makes initiative dangerous?"

Not "why am I too late?" but "what version of me can still appear in the future?"

When the question changes, the field of vision changes. When the field of vision changes, options appear. When options appear, a person stops living inside a single old map. And perhaps that is the moment when life stops ending at eighteen.

## Questions and answers

**What is an image of the future?** It is our inner idea of what is possible, not in the world in general but for "people like me". It works quietly, through fears, borrowed templates and metaphors, and it shapes decisions more than explicit plans do.

**Which cognitive biases narrow the image of the future?** The availability heuristic, confirmation bias, hasty generalisation, anchoring and learned helplessness. Each turns a single painful experience or an unchecked rumour into a map of the whole territory.

**What is a "used future"?** A term from the futurist Sohail Inayatullah: a picture of the future inherited from family, school, culture or media that we live by without ever checking whether it fits us.

**How do you update an image of the future?** Ask where the image came from, what facts confirm or contradict it, and change the question: from "why won't it work for me?" to "how might it be possible after all?".
